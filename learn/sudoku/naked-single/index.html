A Sudoku naked single explained: why A7 must be 7

By Smart Puzzles · Published 5 November 2026

A good Sudoku move has a reason you can check. You do not need to guess when the rules leave a square with only one possible number.

That situation is called a naked single: one empty square, one remaining candidate. Let’s work through a real example.

Find A7 on the board

For this 9×9 puzzle, rows A–I run from top to bottom, and columns 1–9 run from left to right. A7 is the square in the top row, seventh column. It belongs to the top-right 3×3 box.

Here is row A:

1  6  3  2  5  4  _  8  9

The blank is A7. Every completed row must contain each number from 1 to 9 exactly once.

What is missing from the row?

Read the entered numbers carefully: 1, 6, 3, 2, 5, 4, 8, 9. Together, they include every number except 7.

So the row rule leaves only 7 for A7. There is no need to choose between several numbers, and no need to copy an answer from a solved board.

Before placing it, confirm that 7 does not conflict with the other two constraints.

Check the column

Column 7, from row A to row I, is:

_  4  3  9  8  1  2  5  6

It already contains the other eight numbers and does not contain 7. Placing 7 in A7 is allowed by the column rule.

Check the box

The top-right box is:

_  8  9
4  6  _
3  5  2

Its missing numbers are 1 and 7. The box therefore allows 7 at A7 too.

The three checks agree: the row permits only 7; the column permits only 7; and the box permits 1 or 7. Their shared candidate is 7.

Now make the move

Select A7 and enter 7 as a full-size number, rather than leaving it as a pencil note. You have a checked placement: every alternative is excluded, and 7 breaks no row, column or box rule.

Afterwards, review notes in the affected row, column and box. Any 7 notes that now conflict with the placement should be removed. Squares elsewhere on the board are not affected just because they contain a 7 note.

What makes this a naked single?

The conclusion belongs to this square’s candidate list: A7 has exactly one candidate. That differs from a hidden single, where a number has only one available position within a row, column or box, even though that square may contain several notes.

If a square has candidates 2 and 8, you have not found a naked single. Keep both possibilities until another valid deduction removes one.
